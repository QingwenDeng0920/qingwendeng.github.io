Since the reform era, scholars of Chinese politics have puzzled over how an authoritarian regime that concentrates authority at the top nonetheless produces policy outcomes marked by negotiation, delay, and uneven enforcement — the paradox of fragmented authoritarianism. This paper studies China's Central Environmental Inspection (CEI), which moved from an ad-hoc campaign to a routinized, top-down oversight regime piloted in 2015 and expanded nationwide by 2018. We argue CEI is best understood as institutional layering onto heterogeneous local bureaucratic fields: where mandates overlap and horizontal ties are thin, a strong central node redirects effort toward visible, short-term outputs at the expense of medium-run investment, while thicker pre-existing coordination allows the same procedures to lock in cooperation and yield more persistent capacity gains. We construct a city-level Fragmentation Index and exploit the staggered routinization of CEI to separate short-run compliance from medium-run capacity building.
